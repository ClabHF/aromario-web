Top, heart and base notes: what they mean

A perfume doesn't smell the same at five minutes as at five hours. The fragrance pyramid explains why: every scent is a story in three acts.

AROMARIO · Educational guide · 7 min read

If you ever tried a perfume at the store, loved it, bought it, and it seemed different at home, you didn't go crazy: it's the fragrance pyramid in action. Every perfume is built in three phases that unfold on your skin: top, heart and base. Understanding them completely changes how you test and choose fragrances.

The fragrance pyramid: a story in three acts

Think of a perfume like a play. You don't see the ending in the first minute, and judging the play by its first five minutes would be a mistake. It's the same with perfumes: what you smell when you spray is only the beginning.

Act 1: top notes

They're the first thing you perceive, during the first 5 to 15 minutes. They're usually light, volatile ingredients: citrus (bergamot, lemon, grapefruit), green notes, aromatic herbs or fresh fruits.

Their mission is the initial impact: that luminous, attractive first impression. But they're naturally fleeting: they evaporate quickly because their molecules are small and light. That's why a perfume isn't judged by its opening, even though it's what catches the most attention in the store.

Classic mistake: buying a perfume based on how it smells on the store's paper strip after two minutes. You're judging only the top. Wear it on your skin for at least a couple of hours before deciding.

Act 2: heart notes

When the top fades (between 15 minutes and 2–3 hours), the heart emerges: the perfume's true character. Here live the flowers (rose, jasmine, orange blossom, peony), soft spices (cinnamon, cardamom), ripe fruits and more complex green notes.

The heart is what most people perceive for most of the day, and what others notice when they're close to you. If the top is the greeting, the heart is the conversation.

Act 3: base notes

From 2–3 hours on (and up to 8, 12 or more), the base remains: the perfume's foundation. Heavy, tenacious ingredients like sandalwood, cedar, vetiver, amber, vanilla, musk, leather or oud.

The base is what "stays" on clothes the next day and what you remember about the perfume long-term. It's also where ingredient quality shows: a good base is deep and elegant; a cheap one can turn flat or synthetic with the hours.

Why perfumes change on your skin

Everyone has different skin chemistry: pH, temperature, hydration and even diet influence how the notes develop. That's why the same perfume can smell different on you than on your friend, and why paper tests only serve as a first approximation.

The golden rule: always test on the skin, and give it time. A minimum of 2–3 hours to get to know the heart, ideally a full day to discover the base.

How to use the pyramid when choosing a perfume

  • Don't fall in love with the top alone: those sparkling citrus will be gone in 20 minutes. Ask yourself whether you like what comes after.
  • Identify the heart: do you like floral, spicy, fruity perfumes? The heart tells you which family the fragrance really belongs to.
  • Judge the base by your use: if you want a perfume for the whole day, you need a base you like, because that's what you'll be wearing for hours.
  • Beware of heavy bases: an intense oud base can be wonderful at night and overwhelming at the office. Think about when you'll wear it.

The pyramid in practice: an example

Imagine a perfume with a top of bergamot and grapefruit, a heart of jasmine and a base of sandalwood with vanilla. When you spray, you feel citrus freshness (top). Half an hour later, the citrus has softened and an elegant jasmine emerges (heart). Four hours later, a creamy trail of sandalwood and vanilla remains (base). Three perfumes in one, in a single day.

To remember: top = first impression (minutes), heart = real character (hours), base = lasting memory (the rest of the day). Judge a perfume by its three acts, not just the first.

And "notes" aren't literal ingredients

One important detail: when a perfume's notes list says "leather notes", it doesn't mean it contains real leather. Notes describe the olfactory effect: a combination of ingredients that recreates the impression of leather, rain, the sea, or whatever. Perfumery is an art of illusions, and the pyramid is its score.

With this knowledge, next time you test a perfume you'll do it with different eyes (or a different nose): you'll expect its evolution, give it its time, and choose with much more discernment. That, more than anything else, is what separates the impulse buyer from the person who finds their ideal fragrance.
